The 4% rule assumes you quit at 65. Fire removes that assumption

Run the standard retirement calculators and the withdrawal math assumes a thirty-year horizon, because the worker retires at 65 and the plan ends around 95. Retire at 35 and the same money has to stretch across fifty or sixty years. The FireNomics guides treat that difference as the whole ballgame: the famous 4% rule was derived from thirty-year windows, and stretching the window without stretching the portfolio is how plans break.

The failure mode has a name and a shape. Sequence risk: a bad market decade early in retirement does damage that no good decade later can fully undo, because withdrawals lock the losses in while the balance is at its lowest. Over fifty years the probability of hitting at least one brutal opening decade is close to a certainty, so the honest question is not whether the plan survives an average market but whether it survives a specific, rotten first ten years.

The fixes all cost something. A lower withdrawal rate permanently downsizes the budget. A cash buffer softens the first recession but drags on returns for decades. Part-time bridge income in the early years buys the portfolio time, which is why so many long-horizon plans keep one running. What the math will not forgive is pretending a thirty-year study covers a sixty-year retirement.
